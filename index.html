Human-in-the-Loop Escalation

The student calls for help. The platform delivers a human.

The Wellbeing Shift Indicator detects early behavioral change. The Human Guardian Module ensures a licensed professional responds. The escalation path is student-initiated, privacy-preserving, and structurally guaranteed — not dependent on an institution noticing.

1
Wellbeing Shift Indicator fires
The WSI detects a pattern shift in a previously high-performing student — withdrawal, disengagement, changed response cadence — without any change in cognitive markers. The system flags a pre-crisis condition before any institution has noticed.
2
Student initiates outreach
The escalation is always student-initiated. KnoMe surfaces a private, consent-gated prompt — not a report to the school, not a flag on a dashboard visible to administrators. The student chooses whether to reach out and to whom.
3
Human Guardian Module activates
A licensed mentor, counselor, or clinician — already in the student's trusted network — receives a structured, consent-scoped notification. They see only what the student has authorized. AI outputs are reviewed; the human responds directly.
4
Longitudinal feedback loop closes
The interaction is recorded in the student's sovereign pod — not in the institution's system. The family retains the full history. Future risk scoring improves. The community feedback loop, designed by co-inventor John I. Ford, learns across the family network without centralizing the data.
This is not a crisis hotline. It is a pre-crisis architecture — designed to catch the student who goes quiet before anyone else would think to look.

The origin

KnoMe did not begin in 2024.

It began in a free clinic in Maricopa, Arizona, where a physician drove out to give polio vaccinations to migrant farmworkers who could not pay — and came back the next month, and the month after that, for years.

In memory & in honor
Dr. William H. Ford 1924 – 2001
Family physician · Casa Grande, Arizona · 1954–1993
Army medic in England during World War II. Delivered the majority of babies born in the Casa Grande area over four decades. Built the new hospital. Ran a free clinic for migrant workers. Traveled to administer free polio vaccinations. Volunteered monthly at the Arizona Training Program in Coolidge. Served on the governor's board for aging and on the developmental board for Arizona's Medicaid system. Served on the city council. He died March 21, 2001, at 76. He was the inventor's uncle and hero.
Dr. Michael Fallon & Dr. John Fallon
Founders · The Fallon Clinic · Worcester, Massachusetts · est. 1929
The first group medical practice in Central Massachusetts — founded on the conviction that physicians should organize as a team around the whole patient over time. Dr. John Fallon's mother was Ella Ford — the same Ford family line that produced Dr. William H. Ford. The clinic became Reliant Medical Group; the insurance branch became Fallon Health. The inventor is a cousin of Dr. John Fallon.
Ninety-seven years. Three generations of one family. One belief about what medicine owes the person it serves.

KnoMe is the architecture Dr. William H. Ford would have wanted his patients to have — and the architecture the Fallon Clinic would have given its patients if the technology had existed in 1929.

Joseph Ford
Founder & Lead Inventor · Rowboats LLC · Boston, Massachusetts
B.S. International Economics, Arizona State University  ·  MBA, Boston College  ·  M.A. Ethics, Providence College

I earned my B.S. at Arizona State University. I went on to complete an MBA at Boston College and an M.A. in Ethics at Providence College. For two years I worked at a medical ethics think tank — then affiliated with Aetna, CIGNA, and Willis Towers Watson — where I asked questions about the ethics of health data, the ownership of clinical information, and the responsibilities of institutions toward the people whose data they hold. Those questions never left me. They became, decades later, the philosophical architecture of what I built.

I spent years in healthcare and insurance — Aetna, CIGNA, Willis Towers Watson — working at the intersection of health data, institutional decision-making, and individual vulnerability. I then co-founded and ran Academy Learning, an innovative learning center in Sacramento, for ten years. That decade gave me a ground-level education in how children actually learn, where they get lost, and what the difference is between a system designed for institutional convenience and one designed for the child.

Before all of that — in 1982 and 1983 — I spent a formative year in Finland. It was the first of three visits I made to the former Soviet Union and its borderlands during the 1980s. Those years shaped how I understood what sovereignty means to people who have lived without it, and what education means in a culture that has had to fight for the right to teach its own children in its own language.

1989 — Sarajevo

I was traveling through the Balkans and Eastern Europe, headed toward a PhD in East Central European political economy. I was in Sarajevo when Slobodan Milosevic announced the rescission of Kosovo's constitutional autonomy. I watched residents stop in the street. The reaction was not political analysis. It was the physical shock that precedes grief — or war.

I did not fully understand what I was witnessing. I understood it better over the following decade as the wars Milosevic's announcement signaled unfolded across the region. What I saw in Sarajevo in 1989 did not leave me. It became part of the lens through which I later understood what education, mental health, and data sovereignty actually mean in societies where institutions have been used as instruments of suppression — and where the generation born after liberation carries, in their bodies and their families, the weight of what came before.

In 1989 — the same year I witnessed Milosevic's announcement in Sarajevo — Dr. Fatmir Sejdiu co-founded the Democratic Union of Kosovo in direct response to the same event. In 2003 he spent a semester at ASU. In 2006 he became President of Kosovo. He is identified as FAME's founding advisor; outreach is pending.

Between 1995 and 2000, Dr. Pamela Cantor led the Eastern European Child Abuse Project across the precise geopolitical corridor surrounding Kosovo. Her clinical work with traumatized children became the scientific foundation for Turnaround for Children — now integrated into ASU's Center for Whole-Child Education. The clinical line from Dr. Cantor's 1995 Balkans work runs directly to Prishtina in 2026, where I am building with the Kosovo child psychiatrists — Drs. Afërdita Goci-Uka and Mimoza Shahini — who provided emergency mental health services to the children Dr. Cantor was training professionals to serve. Dr. Goci-Uka made a vow during the 1999 NATO airstrikes — sheltering with her children in Prishtina — to become a child psychiatrist if she survived. She did.

My friend Behar Shala was fifteen years old when Kosovo was invaded in 1998. He is now the Director of multiple K-8 schools in Kosovo. His wife is a UK-certified special education teacher. They are not institutional partners in the conventional sense. They are the people who stayed, who built Kosovo's educational infrastructure from nothing, and who understand what is owed to the children growing up in what their generation survived. Behar is the Co-Founder and Executive Director of FAME.

On what is not coincidence

Finland. Sarajevo. A medical ethics think tank. Ten years in a learning center. Decades in healthcare watching institutions make decisions about people's most sensitive information with no accountability to the people whose information it was.

All of that built the architecture I filed a patent on in June 2026. Not because I planned it that way. Because the same questions kept surfacing in different forms, and eventually I had both the time and the tools to answer them.

KnoMe is the attempt to make that covenant technically enforceable.
